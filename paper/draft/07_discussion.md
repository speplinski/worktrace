# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 What the results say about delegation

**The registered question is not settled.** We asked whether an agent's reaction to changes in the evidence is a better basis for delegating a class of decisions than its track record. The registered test passes, and we do not read it as support (§6.1): the advantage rests on one intervention arm, the removal of a register excerpt the policy does not require, whose correct answer a policy audit flagged during the pilot (the second arm the audit flagged, an inverted deed, does not carry it; §6.1), it disappears when the flagged arms are excluded or when the author's reviewed key is used, and in the exploratory multiverse of 60 combinations of four logged policy readings (§8) it reaches a nominal one-sided $p < 0.05$ in ten of the 60 keys, without correction for multiplicity, each of which includes a reading stricter than the legal texts the policy paraphrases; one of the ten is the key that follows the policy's own wording with reporting admitted ($p = 0.011$ on 7 comparisons; §8). Under the 30 further keys of the sensitivity analysis that drops the policy's own independence condition, which the legal text does not contain, it reaches $p < 0.05$ under none (§8). The sensitivity analyses are post hoc, and the reviewed key rests on one reviewer. The registered estimate itself is less certain than the model reports: when the sampling of episodes enters the uncertainty (the two-way bootstrap of §5.7), its one-sided $p$ is 0.052 on all data and 0.033 in the common core, and the 95% intervals reach +0.04 and +0.01. In sum, the pilot cannot tell the two rules apart, and the registered result would have been reported as a success had the answer key not been examined. Among the API configurations, a readiness gate built from only the null arm and the removal of a sole source was never worse than the full intervention family at matched coverage under the registered key and without the contested arms, and split 1 : 1 with it under the reviewed key (§6.2); the pilot gives no evidence that the composed arms add delegation value.

**The key fed both sides of the test.** The certification step and the criterion it was judged by were scored against the same oracles. In the freshness class this produced an advantage that was itself right for the wrong reason (amendment A11, note 6): DSA withheld the class because the agents answered "no action" on an arm whose correct answer the policy leaves open (a file without a register excerpt), and the track record's grants of the same class then looked unsafe because of a different convention that exists only in the oracle code (a filing of annual accounts is not a "change"). A delegation rule inherits every ambiguity in the normative map, and so does any criterion scored against that map. For a delegator the consequence is practical: before two rules are compared, the answer key needs the governance that a model gets. That means an owner, documented readings of every open-textured term, admissible sets rather than single answers where the policy is silent, blind review of a disagreement sample by more than one person, and a sensitivity analysis over the logged readings. In our pilot this review would have cost a fraction of the runs, and it would have been cheaper still before them.

**What the pilot does show about agents.** Right outcomes on a wrong basis occur in every configuration, but they mean different things at the two ends of the range. For the frontier agents between two fifths and four fifths of the basis failures lie on the contested arms, so their extent is a matter of the key (§6.3). For the local and decision models the failures are mostly non-reactions to the decisive evidence (§6.3), the deficit the known-groups check planted in the memorising agent and the instrument detected. Within the two three-size series, the basis loss $\ell_{\text{basis}}$ falls with size in the discrepancy class for the GPT-6 line (32%, 30%, 8%) and in the freshness and onboarding classes for the Claude line (53%, 45%, 42%; 7%, 0%, 0%; common core, calibration split, `paper/pilot/results_core.md`), and not in the other three series–class pairs; with 60 episodes per class these differences are descriptive. Basal 2.0-max's reported probabilities, although overconfident (Appendix A), predict its own basis failures (AUROC 0.84), which suggests that decision models carry information about the basis of their answers that a confidence threshold could use, but the threshold rule cannot certify anything at this sample size.

**Certification at sixty episodes.** With $\alpha = 0.10$ and one failure tolerated, a class is certified only when it is almost clean. Under the registered key DSA certified only the onboarding class, in which escalation is always admissible, so its grants delegated little autonomy; the track record, which counts fewer failures, certified more classes, and more of them went wrong after the shift under the registered key, and none of this survives the reviewed key. A reader should not take a DSA grant in this pilot as a recommendation to automate anything. The sample sizes the design needs for a confirmatory test are those of the second phase.

## 7.2 Why reading explanations does not certify a basis

The judge test gives the pilot's most stable result. Two judges from two developers, one of them a flagship model, read the decision, the explanation and the declared grounds, and both pass most of the correct decisions that fail an interventional test; their Youden's $J$ is near zero, and they rate the explanations of wrong-basis decisions as well as the others (§6.2). Three things follow.

First, an explanation is a report of one execution, and whether a decision depends on a piece of evidence is a relational property that cannot be read from one execution (Pandey et al., 2026b). A judge can check that the stated reasons are consistent with the policy, which is what it did, and that is a different property. Stated reasons that read well and are unfaithful are a known failure mode (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023); the pilot adds that a second model reading the same text passes most of the decisions that fail an interventional test. Second, a judge brings its own reading of the policy. In the onboarding class the Haiku judge read "current register excerpt" as the beneficial-ownership register, unlike the oracles and every agent, and that one reading produced about two thirds of the comparisons the judge-based rules lost (§6.1). A judge-gated autonomy rule, as proposed in delegation frameworks with behavioural metrics (Tomašev et al., 2026) or implemented in readiness qualification with an LLM-judged process defect (Chatrath et al., 2026), therefore adds a second answer key rather than an independent check. Third, the signal that does exist in the trace is structural. The judge's check of the documents the agent named as decisive separated wrong-basis decisions from the rest better than its free-text verdict, and a mechanical comparison with a fixed list of decisive documents did not separate at all (§6.2): what carries the signal is a reading of the declaration against the policy. Declared grounds, as the typed decision records reviewed in §2 propose, are the part of a trace worth keeping, but checking them is itself an act of interpretation.

The limits of this result are the limits of the pilot. There is no third standard: the judge also flags about a sixth of the decisions that pass every intervention, and where the judge and the oracles read a rule differently, as in the onboarding class, the judge may be the one that is right. The judges used one rubric, revised once (A3), and the test of a stronger judge, although its result holds under all three keys, rests on one sample of 199 decisions from five configurations. What we can say is that in this pilot reading the explanation caught few of the basis failures the interventions found, that the pilot offers no substitute for intervening, and that an evaluator who wants that check has to intervene on the evidence or verify the declared grounds against the record, with an answer key that has been reviewed for the purpose.

## 7.3 What a delegated decision costs

The price of model inference keeps falling, yet the total cost of running agents can still grow faster than the price falls. Cheaper decisions invite more of them, more autonomous steps per task and more tasks handed to agents, a rebound effect familiar from energy economics (Jevons, 1865, p. 103). For an organisation the relevant quantity is therefore not the price of a million tokens but the cost of a decision that can safely be left to an agent.

The cost function of §4.7 makes this explicit. Inference ($c^{\mathrm{inf}}_c$) is one term among several, and usually the smallest. A delegated class also carries the expected cost of the errors that the certificate tolerates ($\varphi_c L_c R_c$); a withheld class carries the cost of human review ($e_c + h_c L_c$), measured in minutes of a specialist's time rather than in tokens; and every certified class carries the cost of producing the evidence ($C_c/N$).

The pilot gives a sense of the magnitudes, from the token counts in the run logs (`paper/pilot/token_costs.md`). A factual decision by an agent with tools took three turns and 4,000–8,000 input-side tokens for the API models (Gemma 4 26B, with six turns, 14,000), and a decision model took one call of 1,400–2,600 tokens. Certifying one class from 60 episodes, averaging eight or nine arms per episode, took 7.5–9 times the tokens of the 60 factual decisions. At the list prices recorded in the specification (6 October 2026, checked against the pricing page on 31 October 2026), a factual decision cost between USD 0.0001 (GPT-6 Luna on the Decisions API) and USD 0.035 (Claude Opus 5.5), and certifying one class between USD 0.05 (the Decisions API; USD 0.22 for GPT-6 Luna with tools) and USD 13.69 (Claude Opus 5.5), six to seven times the cost of the factual decisions in the same calibration set for the Claude configurations and eight to nine times for the OpenAI ones (Claude rates as recorded on 6 October 2026, with Sonnet's cache-read rate corrected on 31 October; OpenAI rates as recorded in the run code on 26 October 2026). The local models and the two local decision models ran on the author's hardware and are not priced. The providers' usage exports for the whole pilot (25–31 October 2026) show USD 116.21 billed by Anthropic (Haiku 17.78 including the judge and the smoke test, Sonnet 30.16, Opus 68.27) and USD 23.63 by OpenAI (the three agents, the Decisions API and the second judge); the token counts in the run logs agree with the billed counts to within 0.1% in aggregate, and the logged tokens at the rates above come to within USD 0.04 of the billed total (`paper/pilot/billing_check.md`), so the figures in this section are the billed rates, not a list-price estimate. The cost ratio is below the token ratio because the intervention arms re-read the unchanged documents from the prompt cache, billed at a tenth of the input price for Haiku and Luna and a twentieth for Sonnet, Opus, Terra and Sol at the recorded rates: for the Claude configurations cache reads were 58–81% of the input-side tokens of a factual decision and 86–94% of those of an intervention arm. This cost is paid once per configuration and class and then spread over every decision the certificate covers, plus the cost of sequential monitoring (§4.6). Against a human review of the same file, which takes minutes, it is small.

Two consequences follow. First, the cheapest model per token is not necessarily the cheapest per delegated decision: a configuration that cannot be certified in a class sends that class to human review, and a configuration certified on its track record alone can cost more through errors after a shift. Second, verification has to be priced into deployment from the start, as part of the cost of a decision, not added as an audit afterwards. A delegation rule that certifies the basis of decisions is, in this sense, also a cost-control mechanism: it says where autonomy pays for itself and where it does not.

## 7.4 Applying the instrument where the agent is a product

A reader whose institution has one AI tool, an office assistant such as Microsoft 365 Copilot, will ask how any of this applies. The instrument needs three things from a configuration, none of which is a particular model: it must be callable programmatically, since certifying one class took about five hundred runs in the pilot and monitoring continues in production; its inputs must be controllable, since every intervention replaces one document in the file and the agent must not see the other variants of the same case; and it must return a decision with declared grounds. An assistant used by hand in a chat window meets none of these. An agent built in the institution's own tooling, with the policy as its instructions and a tool that fetches the documents of a case, exposed through that tooling's programmatic interface, meets all three; at the time of writing (October 2026) the Microsoft stack offers this through Copilot Studio agents called from the institution's own code (Microsoft, 2026), and the GitHub Copilot SDK puts frontier models from several developers behind custom tools (GitHub, 2026), the form the pilot's agents took directly on the developers' APIs. What is certified is then the artefact that would be deployed. Two consequences follow from the results above. A product that selects and updates its models silently changes the configuration under the certificate, so certificates are short-lived and sequential monitoring (§4.6) becomes the main instrument. And the convenient reviewer, the same assistant asked to check its own decisions, is the judge of §6.2, which passed most of the wrong-basis decisions it saw. The oracles remain the hard part: an institution has to write down its own readings of its own policy before any agent runs, which is the lesson of §7.1.
