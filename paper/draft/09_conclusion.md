# 9 Conclusion

We asked which evidence should decide that an AI agent may take a class of decisions without review, and we tested one answer, the agent's reaction to interventions on the evidence, against a track record and an LLM judge in a pre-registered pilot with 14 model configurations. The pilot does not show that evidence tests beat a track record. The registered test passed, and the advantage disappeared when one contested intervention arm, the removal of a register excerpt that the policy does not require, was excluded or when the author's review of the oracles' answers was applied; under the combination of policy readings closest to the legal texts the policy paraphrases, there is no advantage. What held under every key is that an LLM judge reading the agent's explanation passes most of the correct decisions that the interventions mark as resting on the wrong basis, and that a stronger judge does no better; the known-groups check confirmed that the instrument detects a planted memorising agent that a track record would certify.

The lesson we draw concerns method. In an evaluation of this kind the answer key is part of the instrument: it feeds both the test and the criterion against which the test is judged, two separately written oracles can share one contestable reading, and a policy written for a benchmark can be stricter than the law it paraphrases without anyone noticing until the results are in. The key therefore needs the governance a model gets: documented readings, blind review by more than one person, admissible sets where the policy is silent, and a sensitivity analysis over readings next to the primary result (§7.1). We publish the registration, every amendment, the ambiguity log with its legal check, the author's review, the multiverse and the code, so that each step from data to conclusion can be checked.

The second phase follows from this: the policy text aligned with the legal texts and every open term fixed in writing before any agent runs; the facts the oracles use visible to the agents; the key reviewed blind by several raters on a sample drawn where models disagree; the delegation question tested with the sample sizes the pilot's variance implies, on new seeds, under a registration written before the data; and one model run both bare, through its API, and behind a commercial agent runtime on the same episodes, to test whether the basis measures depend on the wrapping rather than the model. Whether evidence tests earn a place in a delegation rule is, after this pilot, still an open question, and now one that can be asked with an instrument whose answer key has been reviewed.
