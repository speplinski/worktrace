# 8. Limitations and threats to validity

**What DSA measures depends on the intervention family and on the normative map.** A class is certified relative to the interventions we run and the answers our oracles assign to them. Two independent oracles guard against implementation errors, not against a wrong reading of the policy, and both were written, like the generator, by the AI assistant used throughout (§5.2); versions of a program written independently still fail on the same inputs (Knight & Leveson, 1986). The ambiguity log (`paper/pilot/policy_ambiguities.md`) lists twelve places where the policy text admits more than one reading; six affect the pilot's results. Two were found by a policy audit during the pilot, after interim inspection of the first calibration data (a file without a register excerpt, and an inverted deed that contradicts the excerpt; amendment A1). Two were exposed by the LLM judge: whether review intervals (R3) bind a register-discrepancy decision, and whether the "current register excerpt" of R4 means the business register or the beneficial-ownership register. Two more were raised when the author reviewed a sample of the oracles' answers (amendment A13): whether the documents show that two sources are independent, which exception R2(b) requires (an objection the author withdrew after a later case; §6.1), and whether filing annual accounts counts as a change. The review also judged both readings of the excerpt rule defensible. Under the reviewed key the advantage of DSA in the delegation test disappears (§6.1). We keep primary analyses as registered and report sensitivity analyses. The general lesson is that interventional ground truth is only as good as the policy it encodes, as test labels are only as good as their annotation (Northcutt et al., 2021), so in deployment the normative map needs the same review as the policy itself.

**The policy is stricter than the texts it paraphrases, and that is where the readings diverge.** After the review we checked the disputed readings against the public texts of §5.1; the check, with quotations, is in the ambiguity log. The FATF Recommendations and the EBA guidelines settle none of them. They require "reliable, independent" sources and leave it to each firm to "set out in their policies and procedures which information and data they will treat as reliable and independent" (EBA guideline 4.27), where independence means independence from the customer, not between sources; and they tie re-review to "a change in the customer's circumstances" (4.78) without listing filings. The AMLR, applicable from 10 July 2027, is more specific, and against it the policy is stricter in two of the contested places. Its discrepancy derogation applies when the owners "are known to the obliged entity from another reliable source" (Art. 24(2)(b)); the condition that the source be independent of the register's controlling authority, which the author's review first found impossible to establish from the documents and then accepted as the ordinary reading, is R2(b)’s own. The derogation is also a permission ("may refrain from reporting … and may instead request additional information", Art. 24(2); recital 54 adds that it "should be possible" to let the customer report minor, typographical differences), so reporting stays admissible where it applies, and the oracles' single answer narrows it. Article 26 sets the same review intervals as R3 but triggers an earlier review on "a change in the relevant circumstances of a customer" (recital 69: "material changes"), so R3's "any change recorded in an official filing" goes beyond the text. "Valid proof of registration or a recently issued excerpt of the register" is required only when entering a new relationship (Art. 23(4)); no text requires one in the file of an existing customer or sets its age, so the 30-day window and the excerpt requirement are the institution's choices. R4 follows Art. 76(5), which requires "meaningful human intervention" in decisions to enter, refuse or maintain a relationship taken with AI systems. Among the 60 combinations of the exploratory multiverse (24 distinct keys, because several readings of the missing-excerpt rule give the same answers) (a multiverse analysis in the sense of Steegen et al., 2016), the one closest to these texts (exception (b) available on another reliable source, annual accounts not a change, no excerpt required) gives DSA the lower shifted error in 3 of 9 differing comparisons and the higher in 4 (common core; $\beta_{\mathrm{DSA}} = -0.01$, $p = 0.48$). The key that follows the policy's own wording, which is what the agents read (annual accounts a change, excerpt optional), gives 3 : 2 of 9 ($-0.01$, $p = 0.48$) with the inverted deed scored as the oracles do and 4 : 1 of 7 ($-0.29$, $p = 0.011$) with reporting also admitted; under neither the legal nor the literal reading is the advantage reliable, and the registered key scored agents that reopened a file after an annual-accounts filing as wrong although R3's wording told them to. In the second phase the policy text will be aligned with the legal texts before any test is run. This is our reading of the texts for the pilot, not legal advice.

**DSA could reduce to capability.** If configurations that decide well also always reason well, DSA adds nothing to a track record. In the pilot the two measures do differ, but by how much depends on the answer key. Sonnet 5.5, for example, reached the correct outcome in the discrepancy class while failing an interventional test in 17% of calibration episodes under the registered and the reviewed key and in 5% without the contested arms. The known-groups test shows the same difference in a constructed case: an agent that replays stored answers has zero calibration errors against the oracles, so a track-record rule delegates every class to it, while DSA withholds the two classes in which its answers do not follow the evidence (§6.3). This checks the instrument, not whether the difference helps in delegation; that is what the delegation test tested, and the pilot does not show it (§6.1). The pre-registered construct check found the two measures strongly correlated (§6.3) and still needs more model configurations than the pilot provides.

**Agents may recognise tests.** Some interventions produce unusual files, such as a decisive document missing from an otherwise complete record. An agent that detects such variants could behave differently on them than in deployment. Frontier models already tell evaluation transcripts from deployment ones better than chance (Needham et al., 2025), and our design does not rule out such evaluation awareness. Placebo and sham controls detect sensitivity to irrelevant changes, but not detection of relevant ones. A deployed certifier would need interventions drawn from the same distribution as live episodes.

**Noise inflates basis loss.** Basis loss counts a failure if any of five intervention arms is wrong, so sampling noise adds failures even when the agent's dependence on evidence is correct. Haiku 5.5 changed its answer on 10% of identical re-runs in the freshness and onboarding classes, its three runs disagree on the basis loss in 22% of episodes, and 29% of the variance of its basis loss lies between runs of the same episode (§6.3). Majority voting over its three runs reduced $\ell_{\text{WT}}$ in the freshness class from 53.3% to 47.0% (basis failures from 49.7% to 44.0%), which shows that the noise is real but does not explain the effect. Local models ran once with a fixed seed, so their noise is not measured.

**The baselines are only as strong as we made them.** The judged-process baseline uses Claude Haiku 5.5 for budget reasons. A check with a stronger judge from another developer, GPT-6.1 Sol, on 199 stratified decisions found no better discrimination (Youden's $J$ of −0.07 against +0.07 for Haiku; amendment A10), so a weak judge does not explain the result, but two judges and one rubric do not exhaust the design space. We revised the judge's rubric once, after a 20-decision trial, to stop it from applying rules outside the task (amendment A3). We disclose this and note that the revision favours the baseline. Approval and correction baselines are simulated from oracle labels with a reviewer-error parameter, not collected from human reviewers. All rules but the judged-grounds rule, a heuristic threshold on a mean score, share one certification engine, which favours the simpler rules by giving them the same statistical guarantee.

**The pilot is small.** With three classes per configuration, the delegation test is informative only in the few comparisons in which the rules choose different classes: for DSA against track records, 10 of 28 on all data (11 in the common core) under the registered key and 8 of 28 (9) under the reviewed key, and under the registered key most of these comparisons involve a tie at the boundary of the delegated set (A7). Bonferroni correction across classes is conservative. The pilot can show whether the effects exist and how large they are; the confirmatory delegation test needs the second phase.

**The environment is synthetic.** Documents are short, clean and in English; real files are longer, scanned, multilingual and inconsistent. Control over issuers is signalled only by issuer names. The evidence shift is designed rather than observed, and it is not adversarial: when sources can be corrupted, no procedure that sees the same evidence can avoid deferring some cases to a human (Pandey et al., 2026a). The certification guarantee holds for the calibration generator's distribution; performance under the constructed shift is assessed empirically, and neither says anything about production traffic.

**Certification is not authorisation.** A certified class tells a human what the agent can be trusted to do; it does not move legal responsibility. Decisions that the law subjects to meaningful human intervention, such as onboarding under AMLR Art. 76(5)(b), and those the policy reserves for humans, stay so however high the measured DSA. We paraphrase public rules but make no claim of regulatory compliance.
